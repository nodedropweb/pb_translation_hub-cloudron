Can't Read, Won't Buy — Why Language Matters on Global Websites.

A research study by Donald A. DePalma, Benjamin B. Sargent, and Renato S. Beninatto. Published by Common Sense Advisory in September 2006.


Executive Summary.

Companies large and small devote significant resources to marketing, sales, and product development — even when it comes to incomplete or inaccurate translations. The assumption that potential buyers "probably speak English" drives inadequate localization. The gut feeling that people are unlikely to buy products they cannot understand is well-founded.

This report describes the results of an eight-nation survey conducted in July and August 2006, with over 2,400 consumers answering questions about their behavior and preferences for website visits and purchases — in English and in their own language — across a wide range of product types.

The survey crossed three continents. At least 300 consumers participated from each of the eight countries: Brazil, China, France, Germany, Japan, Russia, Spain, and Turkey.

More than half the sample — 52.4 percent — buys only at websites where information is presented in their language.


Finding One: English-Language Sites Attract Foreign Visitors.

Over two-thirds of respondents — 67.4 percent — visit English-language sites at least monthly. However, nearly one-third spend most or all of their time on native-language sites. People with no or low English confidence are six times more likely to avoid English sites than proficient speakers.


Finding Two: International Visitors Spend More Time in Their Language.

72.1 percent of respondents favor spending most of their time on native-language websites. Language competence is a strong driver: 89.3 percent of respondents with no or low English devote more web time to addresses in their own language.


Finding Three: Consumers Prefer Buying from Sites in Their Language.

48.6 percent of respondents only buy at websites presented in their own language. In France, this rises to 66.5 percent — the highest single-country preference in the study. In Japan, it reaches 65.6 percent. Language creates a higher barrier to purchasing than to mere browsing.


Finding Four: Language Importance Varies by Product Category.

Four out of five consumers — 79 percent — want financial and travel communications in their mother tongue. For high-ticket items like electronics, cars, and appliances, 59.5 percent say their language is important or very important. Even for commodities, over 71 percent consider it important.


Finding Five: All Other Things Being Equal, Consumers Prefer Buying in Their Language.

72.4 percent agreed they would choose a native-language product over a similar product in English, when all else is equal. For consumers with no or low English proficiency, language is 4.8 times more important in purchase decisions compared to their English-fluent counterparts.


Finding Six: More Buyers Will Pay More for Local-Language Products.

56.2 percent are more likely to buy a product with information in their own language, even if a cheaper English-language alternative is available. In China, this preference reaches 90.3 percent — the highest of any nation surveyed. Only 36.9 percent would choose the cheaper product without their language, confirming that language preference outweighs price sensitivity for the majority.


Finding Seven: Mother Tongue Content Becomes More Important Over the Product Life Cycle.

While 45.5 percent say they will only purchase if the user guide is in their language, a much higher 74.7 percent require post-sales support in their native language. Language becomes more critical after the first purchase, not before. 80.6 percent of the no-or-low-English group insist on native-language support after the sale, compared to 70.6 percent of English-proficient users.


Finding Eight: Bad Translations Are Better Than No Translations — and Machine Translation Is Widely Used.

55.9 percent of consumers prefer a poor-quality local-language translation over no translation at all. In Germany, this rises to 65.4 percent. Additionally, 53.5 percent of respondents sometimes use machine translation to navigate English-only websites. As competition increases and consumers have more choices, quick-and-dirty translations will no longer be sufficient.


Why Foreign Visitors Abandon English-Language Websites.

At the entry stage, sites that revert to English too quickly, load too slowly, or overload visitors with animation drive people away immediately. While browsing, excessive information requests, high shipping costs, and confusing legal agreements cause abandonment. At the shopping stage, missing local transaction support and unaccepted credit cards are the main blockers. After registration, shipping costs revealed too late seal the decision to leave. And after purchase, reverting to English for support content and the absence of local support staff destroy long-term loyalty.


Conclusions.

Language of quality drives prospects into the web sales funnel. More complete information in your language keeps them actively browsing. Once they are shopping and buying, a successful global website offers properly adapted forms, transactions, and logistics. A localized experience increases the likelihood of a browser becoming a buyer — and of a buyer progressing to a repeat customer.

For a discretionary purchase, if a visitor sees some benefit to buying, they may tolerate some discomfort to achieve an anticipated gain. For necessary items, people will find and buy the product even from an English-only site — as long as that site allows visitors from their country to complete transactions.

Language drives revenue: 72.4 percent prefer buying in their language when all else is equal. Post-sale retention depends on language: 74.7 percent need native-language support to remain loyal customers. Imperfect beats nothing: 55.9 percent prefer even a flawed translation over silence. And strong global brands can overcome the language barrier in about 56 percent of cases — but local-language content amplifies brand value significantly.

The message is clear: if you want to sell globally, translate locally.
